Full contents: chapters and sections

Key point. In the newspaper database used here, generation terms such as "first-generation idols" appear from the late 2000s, and at that time more than one way of counting generations was in use. The 46 sources gathered here place the generation boundaries differently, but joining the years on which at least two thirds of the sources that classify that year (three or more) agree gives four spans: 1996–2002, 2003–2011, 2013–2018 and 2019 onward (boy groups). The year 2012 settles on neither side. This chapter does not decide which group belongs to which generation.

What this chapter does#

A generation label is a name, not a ranking. So this chapter has no No. 1 list and no comparison of groups or agencies. It looks at three things: when the words for the generations appeared in the press and how they were used, where each source puts the boundaries, and whether market figures differ between the spans so drawn. Group names appear only where a source gives them, in the source's spelling.

The judging rules (numbered R1–R6) were fixed before the data were gathered. Rules on how to record and handle source rows (R0) were added during collection. The rules for handling sources were also changed three times during the calculation: twice when an error was noticed while the calculation was being built, and once after an outside review found a flaw. The working notes say the first two changes came before the judging results were seen; there is no separate record to prove it. The changes are listed at the end of this chapter under "Rule changes."

The words came first#

Year-by-year article counts for each generation term were taken from BIG KINDS, the news database of the Korea Press Foundation, by phrase search (through Nov 6, 2026). The earliest years found in this database are 2006 for "second-generation idol," 2007 for "third-generation idol," 2008 for "first-generation idol" and 2009 for "fourth-generation idol." These are the earliest articles in this database, not first uses; databases differ in the outlets and years they cover.

Because the total number of articles about idols differs from year to year, Figure F8 shows generation-term articles per 10,000 idol articles rather than raw counts. To check that the phrase search catches articles about generations, 23 sample articles were opened; 20 fit (87.0%). The sample was taken at positions fixed in advance among the 100 newest results of each term's busiest year, so it cannot be extended to other years (treated as a simple random sample, the 95% confidence interval would be 67.9–95.5%). Of the three that did not, two matched only in a list of related articles that the site attached, and one had the phrase only in its headline. None used the phrase in another sense. The counts therefore include a small share of such attached text.

The numbering in 2008 was different. An article in the Herald Business (June 2008) named H.O.T., Sechs Kies, S.E.S. and Fin.K.L as the first generation and BoA, TVXQ and Shinhwa as the second, and called the most recent idols after them "third-generation idols." An article in the Seoul Shinmun (September 2008) described Big Bang as "a group that represents the third-generation idols." Yet a table in a 2009 paper by Jung Min-woo and Lee Na-young put Big Bang in the second generation together with TVXQ, Wonder Girls, Girls' Generation and 2PM, and put Shinhwa in the first. Different ways of counting were in use in 2008–2009. In the spans of agreement below, 2007–2011 also falls in the second generation. Counts for "third generation" and "fourth generation" in 2007–2011 may therefore not refer to today's third and fourth generations; the figure marks that span.

Sources disagree#

Forty-six sources that discuss generation divisions were collected in one table. Not all state boundaries as years; some only mention them or defer to other works. The table holds 31 academic papers, 10 news articles, 2 encyclopedias, 2 books or book chapters and 1 critical essay. The news articles were chosen by a rule fixed in advance: a BIG KINDS search for "idol generation division" sorted by relevance, taking the first two qualifying articles in each five-year span; for 2005–2009 the two articles named at the design stage were used, one of which does not meet the criterion of dividing two or more generations and so is not counted.

A criterion that many sources use is an anchor: one group's debut, or one song's release, is taken as the start of a generation. In the table, 15 of the 46 sources record an anchor group or song in the criterion column. Many do not state a criterion at all. Some give two different years for the same boundary (the text and a table disagree, or a debut year and an activity year are mixed); such a boundary was left out of that source's count. Boundaries given only as decades, such as "the late 1990s," are left out of the calculation and not drawn in Figure F9. Their wording is recorded in the boundary table, and part of it also appears in the table under the figure.

Spans of agreement#

For each year, the defining sources that state years were counted by the generation they assign to it. A year joins a generation's span only when at least three sources cover it and at least two thirds of them agree.

First 1996–2002 1996–2002 —
Second 2003–2011 2004, 2007–2011 Girl groups 2003 (62.5%), 2005 and 2006 (60% each)
Third 2013–2018 2013–2018 2012 (boys: third 62.5%; girls: second 50%)
Fourth 2019– 2019– —
Fifth No span Three sources give a start year (two by the same writer), but no year reaches two thirds

The calculation uses 22 sources for boy groups and 22 for girl groups. A source that covers only one sex counts only for that sex; without this rule the girl groups' second generation would run unbroken through 2003–2011, and nothing else would change. In 2012, sources ending the second generation and sources starting the third are divided, and neither classification reaches two thirds. Years without agreement are left out of the spans, so the girl groups' second generation comes in two pieces. This chapter places that year in no generation.

Do market figures differ between the spans?#

For each span (boy groups), the medians of four series were compared: music industry revenue and exports, the share of physical albums, and the combined sales of the year's top 400 albums (Figure F10). The physical share starts in 2013 and the top-400 totals obtained for this chapter cover only 2014–2019, so neither has data for the second span. Three rules apply: is the difference between spans more than twice the usual year-to-year change (R1); does it hold when each source's own boundaries are used (R2); is it larger than the differences found when the series is cut at other years with spans of the same length (R3)?

  • Second to third generation. Median revenue rose from 2.60 trillion won to 5.14 trillion won, and exports from about 22.3 million to 411.8 million US dollars. R1 passes, and the difference holds wherever the series can be split by a source's own boundaries (R2). But keeping the number of years with data (seven before, six after), joining the two spans and moving the cut within 2005–2020 leaves only three other cases (the later span starting in 2013, 2014 or 2015; a start in 2012 is left out because the earlier span would be the same as the actual one), and the one starting in 2014 gives a larger difference for both revenue and exports. The series cannot be said to bend at the generation boundary (R3 not met). All that can be said is that the market was already larger by the time a new generation was said to have begun.
  • First to second generation. Not judged. The Content Industry Survey music data used here begin in 2005, so the first span has no data.
  • Third to fourth generation. Not judged. The Content Industry Survey changed its sampling frame (the list from which respondents are drawn) and linked data sources (other data joined to the survey) from the 2021 reference year, leaving only 2019 and 2020 before the break within the fourth span. The figure does not join 2020 and 2021.

This comparison does not ask whether the generation names changed the market. It asks only whether the years said to mark a new generation match the years when market figures moved most.

Laid over the chart systems#

Figure F11 lays the spans of agreement over the chart-system bands of Chapter 0. The first span falls in the period of broadcasters' ranking programs and Gayo Top 10; during the second span the monthly album sales tally ended and the digital official chart began; from the third span on, everything falls in the Gaon Chart and Circle Chart period. The figure shows which ranking systems each generation was active under; it does not say the systems changed the generations.

Still to check#

  • Two books (a chapter in Aidol edited by Lee Dong-yeon, 2011, and Lee Gyu-tag's 2016 book): the printed pages have not yet been checked. For the first, only the start of the first generation ("around 1996") has been seen, in an excerpt released by the publisher; for the second, it is not yet known whether the book gives generation boundaries as years at all. They are in the table but not counted.
  • One news article is left out because its original page could not be found.

Rule changes#

  • Nov 7, 2026. For the first to third generations, a source that states neither the next generation's start nor an end year counts only the start year. Only the ongoing fourth and fifth generations may stay open-ended. The first calculation counted 2020 as second generation for a paper that gave only "second generation, 2004."
  • Nov 21, 2026. A source that covers only one sex counts only for that sex. The first calculation counted such sources' years for both.
  • Nov 21, 2026 (after review). A span joins only the years that reach agreement. The first calculation filled everything between a generation's first and last agreed years, which put the girl groups' 2005 and 2006 in the second generation without agreement. The same review corrected three rows of the table against the originals (one source's phrase "the third generation, which appeared from 2010 onward" recorded, with 2010 used as its start; one source that gives the third-generation start as both 2010 and 2012 left out of that boundary; one source's anchor statement added).
  • Nov 23, 2026 (after a second review). The calculation and the spans are unchanged. Two rows of the table were completed (the quotation and page for one source's anchor statement and the debut date that paper gives; the "from 2010 onward" quotation for another). In the text, the 2008–2009 comparison of counting schemes, the number of anchor sources, the account of how the rules were changed, the conditions of the moved-cut comparison and what has been checked in the two books were narrowed to what the sources support.
  • Nov 23, 2026 (after a third review). A cell that gives separate values for boy and girl groups is read for each sex on its own. One source gives the fourth-generation start as two years for boy groups and as 2020 for girl groups; the first calculation dropped the whole cell, so the girl-group count took that source's third generation as the single year 2014. After the fix the number of sources per year for girl groups in 2015–2026 rises by one, and the spans are unchanged. Figure F9 does not draw boundaries given only as decades, contrary to the first design, which drew them faintly.
